Sleep

Rebuilding Your Circadian Rhythm After 40

By Paty Straffon

[ ARTICLE IMAGE ]

If you used to fall asleep the moment your head hit the pillow and now find yourself wide awake at 3am, you're not imagining it, and you're not broken. Sleep is the first lever in The 5 Levers for a reason: it's the foundation everything else — energy, appetite, mood, recovery from training — is built on. And in perimenopause, it's usually the first thing to shift.

Why sleep changes now

Estrogen and progesterone both play a role in regulating sleep architecture, body temperature, and the stress hormones that either help you wind down or keep you wired. As those hormones fluctuate, so does your ability to fall asleep, stay asleep, and reach the deep, restorative stages of sleep that repair muscle and regulate blood sugar overnight. Night sweats and a lighter, more fragmented sleep aren't a personal failure — they're a physiological shift, and they respond to a physiological approach.

What actually helps

Circadian rhythm — your internal 24-hour clock — runs on cues, and the two strongest are light and consistency. A few places to start:

  • Morning light, early. Ten minutes outside within an hour of waking helps anchor your clock and improves melatonin release that night.
  • A consistent wake time, even on weekends. Your body clock responds far more to when you get up than to when you go to bed.
  • A cooler room and a wind-down window. Body temperature needs to drop to initiate sleep, which is exactly what hot flashes and night sweats interrupt. A cool room, breathable layers, and dimming lights an hour before bed all work with your biology instead of against it.
  • Protein and strategic carbs at dinner, rather than a very light or very late meal, to keep blood sugar steadier overnight and reduce the 3am wake-ups tied to a glucose dip.

Consistency over a perfect night

You will still have rough nights. The goal isn't a flawless sleep score — it's a rhythm your body can rely on most of the time, so the occasional bad night doesn't derail the week. That's the same principle behind everything in The M Program: small, repeatable inputs that compound, rather than a strict protocol you either nail or abandon.

If sleep is the lever you're struggling with most, it's usually the one worth working on first — better sleep makes the other four levers, from training recovery to appetite regulation, noticeably easier.

Nutrition

Why Protein Timing Matters More Now

By Paty Straffon

[ ARTICLE IMAGE ]

You may already know you should be eating more protein. What surprises most women in The M Program is how much timing changes the payoff — not just how much protein you eat across the day, but how it's spread out.

The muscle-building window shifts

As estrogen declines, the body becomes less efficient at using protein to build and maintain muscle — a phenomenon researchers call anabolic resistance. In practice, that means the amount of protein that used to be "enough" at 30 often isn't enough at 45 or 50 to protect the muscle mass you already have, let alone build more.

The fix isn't necessarily eating more protein overall — it's making sure each meal actually clears the threshold your body now needs to trigger muscle repair, typically in the range of 25–35 grams per meal for most women, rather than saving most of your protein for dinner.

Spreading it out, not front- or back-loading

Most of us default to a light breakfast, a rushed lunch, and a bigger dinner — which means one meal is doing most of the protein work. Three (or four) meals that each hit a real protein target do more for muscle, satiety, and blood sugar stability than one large protein-heavy dinner.

  • Breakfast: eggs, Greek yogurt, or a protein smoothie — not just toast or coffee.
  • Post-strength-training: protein within a couple of hours supports the repair process that's already switched on.
  • Evening: a source of protein at dinner, even if you already hit your number earlier — consistency across the day matters more than any single "best" meal.

It works with the other levers, not instead of them

Protein timing is only one piece. It works alongside strength training (which creates the demand for muscle repair in the first place) and strategic carbs (which spare protein from being used for energy instead of rebuilding). That's exactly why The 5 Levers are built as a system rather than five separate to-do lists.

Why Your 40s Are the Best Time to Start Strength Training

By Paty Straffon

[ ARTICLE IMAGE ]

If you've never lifted a weight in your life, your 40s are not too late to start — they may be the most important decade to start. Muscle mass and bone density both begin declining meaningfully in midlife, and strength training is one of the few interventions that directly slows, and in some cases reverses, both trends.

What's actually happening in your 40s and 50s

Starting in your 30s, adults lose roughly 3–8% of muscle mass per decade, and that rate accelerates through the menopause transition as declining estrogen affects both muscle protein synthesis and bone remodeling. Left unaddressed, this shows up later as reduced strength, slower metabolism, and higher fracture risk — not as an inevitability, but as a predictable consequence of a body that isn't being asked to do resistance work.

Why strength training is the direct answer

Muscle and bone both respond to mechanical load — they get stronger specifically because you've asked them to handle more than they're used to. That's true whether you're 25 or 65. Progressive strength training:

  • Signals bone to maintain and build density, particularly at the hip and spine — the sites most at risk for fracture
  • Preserves and builds the muscle mass that drives your resting metabolic rate
  • Improves balance and coordination, which matters more for fall prevention every year
  • Supports insulin sensitivity, which tends to decline through the menopause transition

You don't need to already be "fit" to start

The biggest barrier isn't physical, it's the idea that strength training is for people who are already strong. In practice, progressions start wherever you are — bodyweight and resistance bands are a completely legitimate starting point, and the adaptation curve for someone new to lifting is often faster than for someone who's been training for years. What matters is consistency and progressive overload: doing slightly more than your body is used to, on a schedule you can actually keep.

This is exactly the theme running through The M Program — every phase is built with bone health and long-term strength in mind, not just an 8-week transformation for its own sake.

Recovery

The Cortisol–Sleep Connection, Explained

By Paty Straffon

[ ARTICLE IMAGE ]

Cortisol gets blamed for a lot in midlife health conversations, not all of it accurate. But its relationship with sleep is one of the clearest and most important to understand, because a disrupted cortisol rhythm and disrupted sleep tend to reinforce each other in a loop that's hard to break without addressing both.

Cortisol is supposed to have a rhythm

In a healthy pattern, cortisol peaks shortly after waking — giving you the alertness to get up and start the day — and gradually declines through the day, reaching its lowest point around bedtime so melatonin and other sleep-promoting processes can take over. Chronic stress, poor sleep, and the hormonal shifts of perimenopause can all flatten or invert that curve, leaving cortisol elevated in the evening precisely when it should be dropping.

Why that shows up as 3am wake-ups

Elevated evening cortisol makes it harder to fall asleep and, for many women, causes a distinct pattern: falling asleep reasonably well but waking abruptly in the early hours, wired and unable to drift back off. That's not a coincidence — it's often a cortisol spike happening at a time it shouldn't, sometimes triggered by a dip in blood sugar overnight.

Breaking the loop

You can't will your cortisol curve back into shape, but you can influence the inputs that shape it:

  • Morning light and movement help reinforce the sharp morning cortisol peak your body needs, which makes the evening decline more likely to follow.
  • Protecting an evening wind-down — dim lights, less screen time, a consistent bedtime — signals to your nervous system that it's safe to lower cortisol.
  • A steady blood sugar going into bedtime, through protein and strategic carbs at dinner, reduces the odds of a middle-of-the-night glucose dip triggering a stress-hormone response.
  • Recognizing that intense, poorly recovered training can itself raise cortisol — which is why Fueling & Recovery is treated as its own lever, not an afterthought to Exercise.

None of this is about eliminating stress or cortisol — you need both to function. It's about restoring the rhythm, so cortisol is high when it should be and low when it should be, and sleep can do the repair work it's meant to do.

Mindset

Consistency Over Perfection

By Paty Straffon

[ ARTICLE IMAGE ]

Every woman who joins The M Program has, at some point, done an "all-or-nothing" health plan — the kind where one missed workout or one indulgent dinner feels like the whole week is ruined, so you stop trying until the next Monday. Consistency over perfection is the coaching philosophy that replaces that cycle, and it's not just a nice phrase — it's a specific approach to how change actually happens.

Why the all-or-nothing model fails in midlife

Perimenopause and menopause bring real variability — sleep that's good one week and terrible the next, energy that fluctuates with hormones you don't fully control, symptoms that show up without warning. A plan that only "works" when everything goes perfectly is a plan that's designed to fail during exactly the weeks you need it most.

What consistency actually looks like

It's not lowering the bar — it's changing what you're measuring. Instead of asking "did I do this perfectly," the question becomes "did I show up in some form, most days." That might mean:

  • A 20-minute session instead of the full 45 minutes, rather than skipping the day entirely
  • Hitting your protein target even on a day when the rest of your eating wasn't ideal
  • Going to bed 30 minutes earlier on a hard week, even if your full sleep protocol slips

Each of these keeps the habit alive. A skipped session breaks momentum; a shortened one doesn't.

It's identity-based, not rule-based

The deeper shift is from following a set of rules to becoming someone who trains, who prioritizes protein, who protects her sleep — because that identity carries you through the weeks a rulebook can't. Rules break when circumstances change. An identity adapts. That's the coaching approach behind every part of The M Program: building the version of you that keeps going, not a checklist you eventually abandon.

If you've fallen off a plan before, it likely wasn't a discipline problem — it was a design problem. A framework built for real, inconsistent life is one you can actually sustain.

Strategic Carbs: Fueling Without Fear

By Paty Straffon

[ ARTICLE IMAGE ]

Carbohydrates get an outsized share of the blame in midlife nutrition conversations, often leading women to cut them further than they should — right at the point when their bodies need them most for training, hormone support, and sleep. Strategic Carbs, the third of The 5 Levers, is about fueling deliberately, not fueling less.

Why cutting carbs too hard backfires now

Carbohydrates support thyroid function, help regulate cortisol, and directly fuel the higher-intensity strength training that protects muscle and bone through midlife. Chronically low carbohydrate intake, especially alongside training and stress, can push cortisol higher and make sleep and energy worse, not better — the opposite of what most women are trying to achieve when they cut carbs.

What "strategic" means in practice

It's not about eating more carbs across the board — it's about placing them where they do the most good:

  • Around training, when your muscles are primed to use that glucose for fuel and recovery rather than storing it
  • Paired with protein and fiber, to slow the blood sugar response and avoid the energy crash that follows a carb-only snack
  • Favoring whole, minimally processed sources — oats, fruit, potatoes, legumes, whole grains — that bring fiber and micronutrients along with the energy
  • Adjusted to your day, with more on higher-activity or higher-stress days and less on rest days — not a fixed number applied blindly every day

Letting go of the fear

For a lot of women, the hardest part isn't logistical, it's psychological — years of diet culture have made carbohydrates feel like something to earn or apologize for. Reframing them as fuel for the strength training and hormone support your body needs right now is often the bigger shift than any specific meal plan.

Strategic Carbs works because it doesn't ask you to fear an entire food group — it asks you to place your energy where your body will actually use it well.

What Bone Density Loss Actually Looks Like

By Paty Straffon

[ ARTICLE IMAGE ]

Bone density loss is one of the quietest health changes in midlife — there's no pain, no obvious symptom, until a fracture reveals how much has already changed. Understanding what's actually happening, and how early it starts, is why bone health is built into every phase of The M Program rather than addressed only after a diagnosis.

The timeline is earlier than most women expect

Bone loss accelerates sharply in the years immediately around menopause, with some women losing up to 20% of bone density in the five to seven years following their final period, as declining estrogen removes a key protective effect on bone remodeling. This isn't a postmenopausal problem to think about later — the steepest drop happens during and just after the transition itself, which makes perimenopause the highest-leverage time to act.

Why it goes unnoticed until it doesn't

Bone remodels constantly throughout life, breaking down and rebuilding in a normal cycle. When breakdown starts outpacing rebuilding, density declines gradually and invisibly — there's no ache, no warning sign, unless you're being screened with a bone density scan (DEXA). The first noticeable sign for many women is a fracture from a fall that wouldn't have broken anything a decade earlier, at which point significant density has often already been lost.

What actually protects bone

Bone responds to mechanical stress — specifically, load and impact that's greater than what it's used to. That means:

  • Progressive resistance training, particularly for the hips and spine, the two most common fracture sites
  • Some impact, where appropriate — jumping, stair climbing, or other higher-impact movement, layered in progressively and safely
  • Adequate protein and calcium, which supply the raw materials for the bone-building side of the remodeling cycle
  • Managing the cortisol–sleep relationship, since chronically elevated cortisol interferes with bone formation

None of this needs to be complicated or intimidating to be effective — it needs to be consistent, and it needs to start now rather than after a diagnosis. That's the entire premise behind building The M Program around bone health from week one.
